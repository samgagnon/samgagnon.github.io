Karma vs. Christ

by Samuel Gagnon-Hartman
21 January 2025

The Vedic epic Mahabharata tells the story of India's mythic past, when Prince Arjuna and his brothers waged war against their cousins for command over the future of India. In one episode of the Mahabharata, a hero named Karna is in transit when he sees a child dying of thirst. Karna stops to give the child water, but his hand slips and he spills his water on the ground. Even so, Karna stoops down to collect the water and give it to the child, saving his life. Later, in the midst of the battle of Kurukshetra, Karna's chariot wheel gets stuck in the mud, dooming him to die under a hail of arrows. He cries out to Prithva, the goddess of the earth, asking why she has cursed him so. She responds that he is reaping the consequences of his own actions, that he is dying because his water was in truth meant for the earth, not the child, that in years' time a date tree would have grown on that very plot of land, and its fruit would have gone on to feed many people. By prioritizing the life of one person in the short term, Karna deprived many others in the long term. Futhermore, in death, Karna will fertilize the earth here that yet new life will spring. Thus is justice served.

When I first heard this story, I was struck by the alienness of karma in its fullest expression: karma includes not only retribution for consequences known to us, but also for those unknown. Vedic religion calls upon us to perform a sort of moral accounting: to keep track of our actions and calculate their consequences. In the Vedic view, our role as moral agents is to effectuate good or negative outcomes in the world, and then whatever we materially recieve, in this life or the next, is in proportion to our own actions.

Jesus Christ instructs us in no such moral calculus. Quite the opposite: should someone sue me, demanding the shirt off my back, Christ commands me to give up not only my shirt but my coat as well, irrespective of the plaintiff's motive. How is this just? I have lost my clothing to someone who sued me out of greed, probably someone who doesn't need my clothes as much as I do. Even so, Christ says, give it up. Lay low your pride, disregarding what may come. It is a radical expression of the First Commandment: I am the LORD thy God, thy shalt have no other gods before me.

So what of Karna? What might Jesus say to him? Obviously we can't know for sure--Jesus famously said a lot of unusal stuff. But given what has been preserved in the Gospels, I imagine that Karna's generosity would be vindicated. The simple act of generosity is a motif which returns again and again in the Bible. In the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus tells his disciples that whatever they do for the least of his brothers, they do for him. If we are to perform the Christian version of the "moral calculus", we might say that Karna still helpled a lone child and deprived many others, but in helping that child he gave aid to Christ himself, an act of undiminishable good.
